# Japanese joint source citations

Page numbers refer to *The Complete Japanese Joinery* (414 pp, scanned PDF
OCR'd locally with Tesseract at 250 DPI). The OCR text is also uploaded as a
source to the related NotebookLM notebook so the names can be queried
directly.

Terminology foundation (p 83):

- **Tsugite** 継手 — end joints (I-axis splices used to extend a member).
- **Shiguchi** 仕口 — right-angle joints (L-axis corners and crossings).
- Common tsugite examples listed in the book: `inro tsugi` (half-blind M&T),
  `koshikake tsugi` (lapped joint), `sogi tsugi` (scarf), `ari tsugi`
  (dovetail), `kama tsugi` (gooseneck), `okkake daisen tsugi`
  (dadoed-rabbetted scarf).
- Common shiguchi examples: `ari shiguchi aigaki`, `watari ago` (dadoed
  cross lap), `hozo sashi` (mortise and tenon).

## Kanawa tsugi 金輪継 — pages 179, 193, 194

> "Half blind tenoned, dadoed and rabbetted scarf joint. Since there is a
> T-shaped half blind tenon at the joint ends of kanawa tsugi and shiribasami
> tsugi, the connecting pieces cannot slide together as in okkake daisen
> tsugi." (p 193)

> "The joint has a 15 mm square pin hole at the center of the joint. When
> the two pieces are placed together, the pin is inserted in the hole to
> secure the joint." (p 194)

Drawing method (p 193): L = 3 to 3.5 × width.

## Shachi tsugi 車知継 — pages 181, 195

> "Key haunch mortise and tenon. Like the kama tsugi (half lapped gooseneck
> joint), the shachi tsugi joint is used where it is impossible to join the
> pieces by sliding them into each other." (p 195)

> "The key slot in both the male and female members is made by cutting a
> slot angled towards the center, or instead of tapering the slot, a tapered
> key may be used." (p 195)

Four slot-cutting methods are illustrated on p 195.

## Koshikake ari tsugi 腰掛蟻継 — pages 83, 122, 134, 190

> "Lapped dovetail joint: this end joint is used on larger members." (p 83)

> "The width of the lap is 15 mm (⅛ of 120 mm). The depth of the lap
> (koshikake) is 15 mm." (p 190)

→ ratio `lap_width = lap_depth = T/8`.

## Shiribasami tsugi / Shippasami tsugi — p 194

> "In certain districts this joint is called shippasami tsugi. Like the
> kanawa tsugi, due to the presence of a T-shaped mortise and tenon, the
> joint has a 15 mm square pin hole at the center. L = 3 to 3.5 times the
> width."

## Mechigai tsugi 目違継 and variants — pp 191, 196, 205–207

> "Mortise and tenon joint. The mortise portion of the joint, mechigai ire,
> is commonly called mechi ire or inro tsugi. The tenon portion of the joint
> is commonly called mechi hozo. Hako mechigai tsugi — blind U-shaped
> stubbed mortise and tenon joint — and kaneori mechigai tsugi — L-shaped
> half blind mortise and tenon joint — are special cases." (p 207)

- **Mechigai hozotsuki kama tsugi** (p 191) — half blind mortised and tenoned
  gooseneck.
- **Hako mechigai tsugi** (p 205) — compared to `kakushi kanawa tsugi` (blind
  dadoed and rabbetted scarf).
- **Jyuji mechigai tsugi** (p 196) — cross-shaped M&T.

## Hako shachi tsugi — p 206

> "L-shaped, dadoed and rabbetted and keyed lapped joint."

## Fasteners — pp 169, 224

> "In order to achieve these standards, kusabi (wedges), shachisen and
> hanasen (keys), and komisen (pins) were used in the past." (p 169)

> "Shachisen key." (p 224, with diagram)

## Context: how the book positions these joints

The book is a carpentry reference, not a cabinetmaking one. Primary use
cases are **mudsills (dodai)**, **girders**, **columns**, **beams**, and
**roof framing**. All structural splices documented above are therefore
designed for members in bending or compression along their length, with the
joint located away from a bending maximum.

Terms that do **not** appear in the book: *Sashimono* 指物 (fine
cabinetmaking) and *Shachihoko* (decorative roof carp). For those, add a
dedicated source such as *The Art of Japanese Joinery* by Kiyosi Seike or
*Japanese Woodworking Tools* by Toshio Odate.
